01 · The Problem

A big audience, a small team, and no machine

When I joined SimplePractice in 2022, the content operation was producing a fraction of what the market demanded. And the audience was the hardest kind to win.

At the time, the platform served 45,000 mental health professionals: clinicians, psychiatrists, and therapists who could spot a generic health article from a mile away and click away just as fast. Winning them meant clearing a bar most content teams never have to touch. Clinical accuracy. Real editorial credibility. A voice that respected how much smarter the reader was than the average blog assumed.

And there was nothing to build on. No AI workflow. No contributor framework. No editorial standards. No system connecting a single published word to pipeline. Just a small team, a demanding audience, and a mandate to make content matter commercially. So I built the machine.

03 · The System

How it actually worked

At this scale, for this audience, you can't manage content piece by piece. You build a machine that runs itself.

Clear inputs. A defined process. Quality gates that catch problems before they ship. Feedback loops that make next month's content smarter than last month's. Here's the pipeline I designed, end to end.

Editorial Pipeline Architecture
01 Strategy
An SEO-led editorial calendar organized around keyword clusters, audience intent, and product priorities. Every slot went to topics with real practitioner search demand and commercial intent, never vanity traffic. The calendar anchored weekly planning with GTM, Product Marketing, and Customer Success.

Keyword research Intent mapping Cross-functional alignment
02 Intake
AI-augmented brief generation using custom Gemini Gems trained on SimplePractice's voice, audience, and editorial standards. Each brief specified target keyword, audience segment, clinical accuracy requirements, word count, internal links, and a suggested outline, cutting brief-writing time by roughly 70%.

Gemini Gems Custom AI assistants Templatized briefs
03 Production
A tiered contributor network of 150+ freelancers segmented by expertise: licensed clinicians for accuracy, SEO specialists for high-volume content, senior editors for thought leadership and executive ghostwriting. Each tier had its own brief format, rate card, revision policy, and quality rubric, plus real onboarding built on brand immersion and voice training.

Tiered contributors Clinical experts Onboarding system
04 Quality
Multi-gate editorial review. An AI-assisted first pass caught voice, tone, and SEO issues. Editors owned clinical accuracy and narrative quality. I was the final gate: the call on what shipped, what got killed, and what went back for rework. If it could have run on any other health platform, it didn't run on ours.

Editorial rubrics Clinical review Voice compliance
05 Distribution
A multi-channel distribution playbook. Nothing published and died. Every piece had a downstream life: blog, email newsletter, social, sales enablement, or customer story. High-value content was timed to product launches and campaigns in partnership with GTM and Communications.

Blog Email Social Sales enablement
06 Measurement
A content attribution framework tracking organic traffic, keyword rankings, time-on-page, lead conversion by content type, and paid-account attribution. Weekly reviews fed straight back into prioritization: content that converted got scaled, content that didn't got cut or refreshed.

SEO analytics Lead attribution Conversion by type

The goal was never to produce more content. It was to build a system where quality and volume stopped competing.

The operating principle
04 · The AI Layer

AI didn't replace the work. It augmented the workflows.

By 2025, AI touched 75% of our content. But it was always a tool inside a human-built system, never a substitute for editorial judgment.

The real breakthrough was the custom Gemini Gems: AI assistants fine-tuned on SimplePractice's voice, clinical vocabulary, and audience expectations. These weren't generic prompts anyone could copy. They were trained editorial collaborators that understood the difference between how a therapist talks to a client and how a platform talks to a therapist. That distinction is one most AI content misses completely.

The payoff: a two-person in-house team that shipped like a team three times its size, without ever giving up the clinical credibility that made the content worth reading in the first place.

05 · What I Learned

Five things I learned from building an AI-augmented content engine

01
The bottleneck is the brief, not the writer. When quality is inconsistent, the instinct is to edit harder. Usually the brief just wasn't specific enough. Investing upstream in brief quality is the highest-leverage move in all of editorial.
02
Treat contributors like partners, not vendors. The best freelancers weren't just delivering copy. They were invested in the product. That only happens when onboarding is real, feedback is fast, and good work leads to more meaningful work.
03
AI exposes weak editorial judgment, it doesn't fix it. The teams that struggled with AI weren't fighting the technology. They were fighting a lack of clarity about what "good" looked like. AI just made that ambiguity visible faster.
04
Credibility is a moat. While every health platform churned out generic wellness content, choosing clinical depth over volume built a durable edge. The audience noticed. So did the algorithm.
05
A system beats a hero. The point was never to be the person who could write anything on demand. It was to build something that produced excellent work whether or not any single person showed up that day. That's the difference between running a team and building a machine.
